# Case Studies: Problem–Solution–Outcome Narrative

When evaluating case studies, a FirstClick analyst asks: does the narrative establish a readable chain of problem, context, actions taken, and outcome in the corpus, or is it just a long advertisement? This document is the standard framework for case study evaluation in the FirstClick knowledge base. [kb:102-case-studies]

## Scope

This file covers: case structure (context → challenge → solution → outcome), measurable vs qualitative results, segment matching, quote and visual evidence, bridge to CTA, and sales enablement usage. Out of scope: short social proof strips (see [kb:101-social-proof]), procurement packages (see [kb:104-procurement]). A case study is in-depth evidence; it is not a logo wall.

Heuristic: A good case makes the reader say, “this sounds like our story,” and explains how the outcome was achieved without exaggeration. No made-up percentages or ROI; if there are numbers in the corpus, they are quoted; if not, qualitative results suffice.

## Diagnostic Signals

1. **Attempt at advertisement**: Only praise sentences; no problem definition.
2. **No context**: Sector, scale, previous tool not specified.
3. **Outcome fog**: “Significant improvement”—what improved, how it was measured is missing.
4. **Fake-sounding exact percentages**: Analyst does not invent; if the product claims suspiciously precise figures, note “no verifiable source.”
5. **Single persona blindness**: Only C-level quotes; no daily user experience.
6. **Outdated stack**: Case is three years old; product feature no longer exists.
7. **Broken CTA**: Case ends, but “what to do for similar results” is missing.
8. **Privacy contradiction**: “Anonymous Fortune company” + overly specific metric undermines credibility.

Positive signals: clear starting state, selection criteria, implementation steps (brief migration note), qualitative or sourced quantitative results, relevant role quotes, similar segment filter, PDF/one-pager summary.

## User Objections

- **“Is this our size?”** Scale mismatch.
- **“Is the result exaggerated?”** Unsourced percentage.
- **“How long did implementation take / who worked on it?”** Switching cost hidden.
- **“Any failures or limitations?”** Suspicion of one-sided perfection.
- **“How was security / GDPR handled?”** Enterprise reader.

## Persona Reactions

- **busy-professional**: Wants a one-page summary + 3-point outcome; won’t read a 2000-word PDF.
- **non-technical**: Wants a jargon-free “before / after” narrative.
- **skeptical**: Looks for methodology and limitations; “everything is perfect” text breaks trust.
- **price-sensitive**: Needs cost and plan context; only enterprise success stories are not convincing.
- **student / first-timer**: Mini case or template success story is more suitable; heavy case study may be overkill.

## Good and Bad Examples

**Bad — praise wall**
“Company X loves our product. Great support. Highly recommended.” No problem/solution.

**Good — chained narrative**
Context: 25-person e-commerce operation, tracked in spreadsheets. Problem: inventory mismatch. Solution: which module of the product, in how many weeks. Outcome: “daily inventory meeting eliminated” (qualitative)—if there’s a number, quote from corpus.

**Bad — number theater**
“400% ROI, 90% faster”—no source, period, or baseline.

**Good — honest result**
“In the first month, we shortened invoice processing time according to internal measurements” + customer quote; or just qualitative: “the finance team reported reduced end-of-month panic.”

**Bad — wrong shelf**
Marketplace case study on HR product’s pricing page.

**Good — contextual placement**
On the relevant solution page, with similar sector filter.

## FirstClick Score Effects

- **trust**: Well-structured case strengthens trust rationale; suspicious numbers decrease trust.
- **clarity**: Case concretizes what the product does; without it, the promise remains abstract.
- **adoption**: Indirect—makes internal selling by the B2B champion easier.
- Timeline: Write “case quality: weak/strong” and the missing link (context/outcome).

## Action Checklist

1. Inventory case pages; check structure links.
2. Compare segment fit with target buyer.
3. Scan quantitative claims for sources; warn if missing.
4. Check for migration/time notes.
5. Distinguish from social proof strip: is depth sufficient?
6. Add persona objection.
7. If sales-led, suggest PDF summary for committee.
8. Suggest a one-sentence improvement.

## Deep Diagnostic Scenarios

**Scenario A — Metric theater.** Big percentages, no baseline or period. Skeptical persona rejects; analyst does not repeat percentage, writes “unsourced quantitative claim.”

**Scenario B — Implementation fog.** Outcome present; who implemented, how many weeks, which module missing. Busy-professional cannot estimate effort.

**Scenario C — Only happy ending.** No limitation, trade-off, or “still manual part.” Overly polished narrative may weaken trust.

**Scenario D — Showcase without CTA.** Case ends; reader cannot find next step (demo, similar template, contact).

**Scenario E — Wrong product surface.** Case is about feature X; page is selling feature Y.

**Scenario F — Anonymous with excessive detail.** “Unnamed bank” + very specific internal process—credibility tension; note it.

## Mini Template (can be added to suggestion text)

For a weak case, the analyst can suggest this skeleton (without inventing content): Context (sector/scale) → Problem (previous state) → Why this product → Actions taken (module/steps) → Outcome (qualitative or sourced quantitative) → Quote → CTA. The template is instructive as a standalone RAG chunk.

## Case Study vs Social Proof

A quote is a one-sentence trust transfer; a case study approaches a causality claim. In FirstClick findings, distinguish between “weak social proof” and “missing case chain.” In B2B sales-led, the case is part of the champion’s file ([kb:100-sales-led-b2b]). In PLG, a short case also works next to pricing or empty state.

## Common Analyst Mistakes

1. Making up non-existent ROI.
2. Counting every long customer story as a good case—if there’s no structure, it’s an ad.
3. Presenting old cases as current evidence.
4. Ignoring migration effort ([kb:105-switching-migration]).
5. Inventing numerical scores like “add case study, trust +20.”

## RAG Independent Chunk Note

Chunk questions: is there context; is the problem–solution–outcome chain established; is the quantitative claim sourced; is the segment a match? Preserve the terms context, challenge, outcome, qualitative result.

## Turkey and Language Notes

In TR cases, explicitly stating scale and sector is critical for SME buyers. If there is no Turkish case but the product is sold in TR, note the gap. Currency and plan names should pass drift control with current packaging.

## Reading Time and Chain Breaks

Heuristic: executive summary on top, details below. Chain breaks: outcome present, no problem; problem present, no solution; solution present, no outcome; quote present, no context. Write each break as a separate item.

## Positioning

In sales-led, the case is part of the committee kit. In PLG, a short case can sit next to pricing or empty state. If CTA is demo, check for sales-led alignment; if self-serve, check for PLG file alignment.

## FirstClick Report Paragraph

“Case [title/URL quote]. Chain: context [present/absent], problem [present/absent], solution [present/absent], outcome [qualitative/unsourced quantitative/absent]. Segment [fit]. Suggestion: [skeleton item]. Score: trust/clarity — case study quality.”

## Evidence Usage Matrix

Pricing page: short outcome + similar scale. Security page: process evidence, not case. Demo page: full chain. Empty state: mini success story or template. Wrong matrix cell produces confusion.

## Value of Admitting Limitations

Phrases like “still manual,” “migration took X weeks,” “only Y module used” can increase credibility for the skeptical persona. Do not invent if not in corpus; if present, mark as a positive signal.

## Common Corpus Contradictions

Case says “migration in two weeks”; migration page requires professional services—surface and duration contradict, note the contradiction without inventing. Case describes Pro plan success; CTA is only for Enterprise demo. Feature in outcome is named differently in UI; jargon drift requires citation. “Anonymous customer” has both very specific metric and anonymity—credibility tension. Same customer appears in three different cases with copy-paste praise; illusion of variety. Analyst writes each contradiction as a separate finding line and adds relevant kb cross-reference (migration, pricing, sales-led).

## Segment Matching Rubric

The analyst qualitatively reads the match between case and target buyer on four axes: sector (same/adjacent/distant), scale (micro/SME/enterprise), usage type (self-serve/team/enterprise), and maturity (first-time/switch/expansion). The further the match, the weaker the case’s persuasive power; analyst writes this as “segment drift” and, without inventing percentages, says “weak evidence for this buyer.” Example: a case for a 500-person bank shown to a solo freelancer shifts on two axes—scale and usage type—double drift strengthens the finding. Conversely, adjacent sector + same scale shifts on only one axis; analyst marks this as “acceptable proximity” and does not count the case as entirely invalid.

## Evidence Chain Scoring Example (Qualitative)

FirstClick does not use a numerical score formula; but the analyst can count how many links in the case chain are evidenced. Links: context, problem, selection rationale, implementation, outcome, quote, CTA. If six out of seven links are concrete and the source of the outcome is clear, trust rationale is strengthened; if only outcome and quote are present (five links missing), analyst writes “not a case, just a showcase.” This count is not converted to a score; instead, note on the timeline as “case chain: 6/7, missing CTA.” Naming the missing link makes the suggestion more concrete.

## Edge Cases

- **Founder’s own case**: Not an independent customer; tag as “internal example,” do not count as social proof.
- **Anonymous due to NDA**: Anonymity is legitimate; but if it contradicts with overly specific metrics, note it.
- **Award-winning but old case**: Award is a trust signal; if product feature no longer exists, add freshness warning.
- **Video-only case**: If there is no text transcript, RAG and accessibility are weak; mark as “no searchable text.”

## Persona Objection Dialogues (Sample Language)

busy-professional: “Can I forward a summary of this to my manager, or do I have to read 12 pages?”
skeptical: “Where did this percentage come from, is there a period and baseline?”
price-sensitive: “Which plan is this success on, and is it close to our budget?”
non-technical: “What will I do during migration, is a technical team required?”
The analyst does not fill these questions with made-up answers; checks if there is a match in the corpus. If not, it is a finding of deficiency. Dialogues carry separate chunk value for objection scanning in RAG.
